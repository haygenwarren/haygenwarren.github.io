When you look at a star, you are not seeing it as it is now. You are seeing it as it was — years, decades, centuries, or millennia ago, depending on how far its light had to travel to reach your eye or your detector.

This is one of the stranger and more beautiful facts of observational astronomy. Every observation is a kind of time travel, though you are not the one moving. The photons are. They left their source at the speed of light and have been traveling ever since. By the time they reach you, they carry information about a moment that has long since passed.

In practice, this means that a single image from a telescope contains objects at many different distances — and therefore many different epochs. A galaxy a hundred million light-years away appears as it was a hundred million years ago. A quasar at cosmological distances may be showing us the universe as it was when life on Earth had not yet begun.

This is the peculiar geometry of the observable universe: we look outward into space and backward into time simultaneously. The further we look, the earlier we see. The cosmic microwave background, the faint afterglow of the Big Bang, is the oldest light we can detect — released roughly 380,000 years after the universe began, when it cooled enough for protons and electrons to combine into neutral hydrogen and become transparent.

There is something worth sitting with here. The act of observation, which seems so immediate and present, is always in some sense an encounter with the past. This is true even of everyday life — light from a lamp takes a fraction of a nanosecond to reach your eyes — but at astronomical scales the delay becomes unmistakable and profound.

I find this makes looking at the night sky a more layered experience. Each point of light is a message in transit. Some of those sources may no longer exist. We are seeing, in a very literal sense, what was.
